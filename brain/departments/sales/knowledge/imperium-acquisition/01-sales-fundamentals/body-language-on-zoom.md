Module 1, Section 5 of the Imperium Acquisition sales curriculum.

## Overview

Sales did not stop being a physical, in-person skill when it moved to video. It adapted. The same
signals that communicate confidence, interest, and authority in a face-to-face meeting still
operate on a Zoom call, they are just mediated through a camera and a screen. Most reps have
never thought carefully about how they look on video, and it shows.

This lesson covers the specific physical elements that matter most on a video call: camera
setup, posture, eye contact, stillness, and what nervous body language looks like so you can
correct it. These are controllable variables. Getting them right costs nothing and makes a real
difference to how the prospect experiences you.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

- Set up your camera and framing to project competence and confidence.
- Understand what eye contact through a camera actually means and how to do it.
- Use posture and stillness as active signals of authority rather than passive defaults.
- Identify what nervous body language looks like on video and correct it.
- Understand how background and lighting contribute to first impressions.

## Theory

### Non-Verbal Communication Does Not Disappear on Video

Mehrabian's research on the weight of non-verbal communication in face-to-face interaction
applies in adapted form to video calls. While the full range of body language is compressed into
a smaller frame, the signals that are visible carry proportionally more weight because the
prospect is staring at a small screen with you as the central focus. They will notice stillness or
fidgeting, confident posture or a slumped position, eye contact or evasion, far more acutely than
they might in a room.

### Eye Contact Through a Camera

On a video call, real eye contact means looking into the camera lens, not at the prospect's face
on your screen. Most people look at the face on their screen, which means on the other end, you
appear to be looking slightly downward and away. This reads as evasion or distraction.

Training yourself to look at the camera, particularly during key moments such as when you are
asking a question, stating price, or making a recommendation, creates the impression of direct
eye contact and signals engagement and confidence. It feels unnatural at first because you
cannot see their reaction, but the impact on the prospect is significant.

> A simple fix: position a small sticky note or arrow just below your camera lens as a reminder to
> look there rather than at the screen. After a few calls, it becomes automatic.

### Posture and Stillness

Stillness is a signal of composure. A rep who is calm and confident tends to be physically still,
they are not rocking in their chair, tapping fingers, constantly adjusting position, or leaning in and
out. These movements read as restlessness or anxiety, and the prospect picks them up
subconsciously even if they could not articulate why they felt uneasy.

Sitting slightly forward, with a straight back and both feet on the floor, conveys attentiveness and
confidence simultaneously. It is a posture associated with active engagement, not passive
observation, which is exactly the role you want to occupy on the call.

### Camera Angle, Framing, and Background

A camera positioned below eye level forces the prospect to look up at you slightly, which
subconsciously registers as looking up at someone in authority. This is the opposite of what you
want. Camera at eye level or very slightly above is the target: it creates an even, composed
framing that signals equality and professionalism.

Background and lighting are trust signals. A cluttered, dark, or chaotic background creates
subliminal noise that distracts from you and suggests disorganisation. A clean, well-lit setup
takes minimal effort but signals that you are serious about what you do. Good lighting, ideally
from the front, means the prospect can read your face clearly, which makes connection easier.

## Application

### Camera at eye level

Stack books, use a laptop stand, or raise your monitor so the camera is at eye level. This takes
five minutes to fix and has an immediate effect on how you are perceived on every call
thereafter.

### Light from the front

Sit facing a window or a ring light. Avoid light sources behind you, which turn you into a
silhouette. Clear, front-facing light means the prospect can see your face well, which builds
connection and trust.

### Clean your background

You do not need a studio setup. You need a wall or a tidy corner. A neutral background keeps
the focus on you. If your space is difficult, a simple virtual background can work, but a real,
clean background always looks more professional.

### Audit your own video

Record a mock call or a real one and watch it back with the sound off. What do you see? Are you
still or fidgeting? Is your posture composed or slumped? Are you looking at the camera or the
screen? Most reps are shocked by what they see when they look at themselves objectively for
the first time. Use it.

> Your setup is a signal about your standards. A prospect is forming an opinion about whether to
> trust you with their business. The physical environment you bring to the call — framing,
> lighting, background, posture — is part of that data. It costs almost nothing to get it right.

## Key Takeaways

- Non-verbal signals still carry significant weight on video calls.
- Real eye contact on Zoom means looking at the camera lens, not the screen.
- Stillness reads as composure; fidgeting reads as anxiety.
- Camera at eye level, front-facing light, and a clean background are basic professional
  standards.
- Record yourself and audit your own body language with the sound off.
