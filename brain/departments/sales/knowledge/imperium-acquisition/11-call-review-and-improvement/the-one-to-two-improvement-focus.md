## Overview

The most common reason that reps who review their calls do not improve
as fast as they should is that they try to fix too many things at once.
Each call review surfaces five problems. They commit to working on all
five. On the next call, none of the five has been addressed properly
because attention was divided across all of them.

Improvement is a sequential process. You get better at one thing at a
time, in the same way that physical skill development works. This
lesson introduces the one-to-two focus principle: picking the most
impactful thing to work on this week, practising it deliberately until
it is solid, and then moving on.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

- Select the right improvement focus from the options your call review
  surfaced.
- Practise a specific skill deliberately rather than hoping general
  effort produces improvement.
- Track what you changed and what the result was.
- Know when a skill is solid enough to move on from.
- Understand the compounding effect of sequential focused improvement.

## Theory

### Why Trying to Fix Everything Fixes Nothing

Divided attention produces divided results. When a rep tries to improve
five things simultaneously, they are monitoring five things during every
call. That monitoring consumes cognitive bandwidth that should be going
into listening, reading the prospect, and responding in the moment. The
call gets worse, not better.

The principle of deliberate practice, developed by psychologist K.
Anders Ericsson, shows that expert performance is built through highly
focused practice on specific, targeted skills — not general repetition.
A rep who runs fifty calls while consciously practising the confirmation
frame will master the confirmation frame faster than a rep who runs
fifty calls while trying to improve five things at once.

### Picking the Right Focus

The right focus is almost always the one that is furthest upstream in
the call. If the backward trace consistently points to discovery, work
on discovery. If discovery is solid but transitions are abrupt, work on
transitions. Move from the beginning of the call toward the end: the
upstream improvements create the conditions for downstream improvements
to follow.

The wrong focus is the one that feels most urgent but is actually
downstream of a more fundamental problem. If close rate is low, the
answer is rarely to practise closing. It is usually to go further back.

> A rep who improves one thing per week, specifically and deliberately,
> is a meaningfully better rep in three months. A rep who tries to
> improve everything simultaneously is often at the same level.
> Sequential focus compounds. Scattered effort does not.

### The Improvement Log

Keep a simple log: the focus area, the specific change you made, and the
result across your next five to ten calls. This log does two things: it
makes the practice intentional (writing it down creates accountability)
and it produces evidence of what works (which is useful when you are
deciding what to focus on next).

## Application

### Pick one thing from your most recent review

From the call review you just ran, pick one improvement. Not the most
interesting one — the most impactful one. Write it down: "This week I am
working on [specific behaviour] because [specific reason from call
review]."

### Set a measurable target

What would "improved" look like for this skill? Be specific: "I will
hold silence for at least five seconds after price on every call," or "I
will ask a follow-up question on every answer in discovery before moving
to the next area." Measurable targets make practice deliberate rather
than vague.

### Do not move on until it is automatic

The signal that a skill is solid enough to move on from: you can execute
it without consciously monitoring it. When the confirmation frame has
become something you just do rather than something you have to remember
to do, you are ready for the next focus.

## Key Takeaways

- Trying to improve five things simultaneously fixes none of them.
- Deliberate practice on one specific skill beats general repetition
  every time.
- Pick the focus furthest upstream — that is where the biggest
  downstream leverage is.
- Keep an improvement log: what you focused on, what you changed, what
  the result was.
- Move on when the skill is automatic, not when the week is over.
