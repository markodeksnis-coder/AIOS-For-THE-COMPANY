## Overview

Most reps who review their calls listen for the wrong things. They listen
to the close, because that is the moment they remember most clearly and
because it is where the outcome was decided. They skip the first twenty
minutes, even though that is where the outcome was actually determined.

This lesson gives you a framework for listening to calls in a way that
produces genuine insight: where to start, what to listen for, and how to
turn what you find into a specific improvement rather than a general
feeling that the call could have gone better.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

- Apply the call review framework: control, discovery depth, transitions,
  close.
- Listen to calls from the beginning rather than jumping to the end.
- Identify specific, actionable improvement points rather than vague
  observations.
- Establish a consistent review habit with the right frequency.
- Distinguish between what you intended to do and what you actually did
  on the call.

## Theory

### What Most Reps Get Wrong

Listening to a call from the end backwards produces a bias toward
late-stage behaviours. The rep reviews their close, evaluates whether
they handled the objections well, and draws conclusions about what to
fix. But the objections and the close quality are almost always
determined by what happened in the first half of the call. Reviewing
from the end hides the actual cause.

The other common error is reviewing for what you hoped you did rather
than what you actually did. Reps listen to their discovery and hear
themselves asking questions. What they often miss is the quality of what
they asked, whether they followed up on the answers, and whether the
conversation went deep enough to establish genuine urgency.

### The Review Framework

**Control:** from the first thirty seconds, did you establish the frame
for the call? Did you set the agenda? Did you maintain control
throughout, or were there moments where the prospect took over the
direction of the conversation?

**Discovery depth:** did the questions go below the surface? Were the
answers followed up? Did the cost of inaction get named and quantified?
Was there a specific problem on the table by the end of discovery, or
only a general dissatisfaction?

**Transitions:** did the call move cleanly between stages, or did it jump
abruptly? Did the diagnostic summary happen? Was there a confirmation
before the pitch?

**Close:** how was price delivered? Was silence held? Was there a direct
close question?

> The most useful call review is not "how did I perform?" It is "where,
> specifically, did this call deviate from how it should have been run?"
> That question has a concrete answer. The first question does not.

### Frequency and Format

The right frequency for call review depends on experience level. Reps
who are developing their fundamentals should aim to review two to three
calls per week. Reps who are more experienced can do less — but should
review any call that did not produce the expected outcome. Calls that go
well are also worth reviewing: understanding what worked is as important
as understanding what did not.

## Application

### Always start from the beginning

Listen to the first 60 seconds of every call you review. That is where
the tone, the control dynamic, and the framing of the conversation are
established. Everything you find later in the call has its roots there.

### Score each framework element

For each of the four framework elements — control, discovery depth,
transitions, close — give yourself a simple rating: strong, adequate, or
needs work. That gives you four specific data points rather than a
single overall impression.

### Write down one thing to change

After every review, write down one specific thing you will do
differently on the next call. Not five things — one. Focus is what
produces change. Over ten reviews, you will have worked through ten
specific improvements. That compounds.

## Key Takeaways

- Review from the beginning — not from the end. The close reflects the
  earlier call quality.
- Listen for what you actually did, not what you intended to do.
- The four framework elements: control, discovery depth, transitions,
  close.
- Score each element specifically — strong, adequate, needs work.
- Write one specific change after each review. Focus on one thing at a
  time.
