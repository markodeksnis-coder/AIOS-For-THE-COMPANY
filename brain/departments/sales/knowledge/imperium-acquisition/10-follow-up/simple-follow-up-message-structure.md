## Overview

Most follow-up messages are too long, too vague, or both. They either ramble through a recap of
the call, or they say almost nothing. Neither approach produces responses. The follow-up
message that works is short, specific, and built around three components: a reason, a
reference, and a next step.

This lesson breaks down that structure and provides the templates for the scenarios you will
encounter most often. Not scripts — structures. The content changes based on the call. The
shape stays the same.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

- Construct a follow-up message using the reason-reference-next step structure.
- Keep follow-up messages short enough to be read and acted on immediately.
- Adapt the structure to different follow-up scenarios.
- Identify the common mistakes that make follow-up messages ineffective.
- Build a small library of follow-up structures for your most common situations.

## Theory

### The Three Components

Every effective follow-up has a reason: why you are contacting them now, connected to
something specific from the call or from their situation. It has a reference: a direct callback to
what was discussed, using their language, that demonstrates you have been thinking about their
situation. And it has a next step: a clear, single, low-friction action they can take in response.

Without the reason, the message feels arbitrary. Without the reference, it feels generic. Without
the next step, the prospect does not know what to do with it and defaults to nothing. All three
components are required for the message to produce a response.

### Keep It Short

Follow-up messages should be readable in under 30 seconds. If they take longer, they will often
not be read at all — or they will be deferred, which is usually the same outcome. The instinct to
include everything in a follow-up is a version of the same instinct that produces over-pitched
calls. It communicates anxiety, not value.

Three to five sentences is the target. Enough to cover the three components, nothing more.

> "Hi [Name] — following our call, I have been thinking about what you described around
> [specific problem]. Given the timeline you mentioned, I wanted to check in: where are you in
> working through the decision? Happy to answer any questions that have come up." Three
> sentences. Reason. Reference. Next step.

### Templates for Common Scenarios

- **"Not yet decided"**: Reference the call, acknowledge the timeline they gave, ask a single
  direct question about where they are in the process.
- **"Went quiet"**: Reference the call, name one specific thing from it that is relevant to their
  current situation, ask whether it is still a priority for them.
- **"Said they would be in touch"**: Acknowledge that you said you would give them space,
  reference the specific thing they were going to work through, offer a specific prompt to move
  the conversation forward.

## Application

### Write your three-scenario templates

For the three most common follow-up situations you face, write a template that fits the
reason-reference-next step structure. Keep each one under five sentences. Test them over the
next month and refine based on response rates.

### Measure response rate by message type

If you are sending follow-ups consistently, you have data. Which message types produce
responses? Which produce silence? The differences will tell you which elements are working and
which are not.

### Never send a follow-up without a specific next step

Every message should give the prospect one clear thing to do: respond to a specific question,
confirm a time, or review a specific piece of information you shared. Vague messages produce
vague responses. Specific prompts produce action.

## Key Takeaways

- Every follow-up needs three components: a reason, a reference, and a next step.
- Keep it under five sentences — if it takes more than 30 seconds to read, it will often be
  deferred.
- Use the prospect's language in the reference — it signals you were listening.
- A specific next step is the difference between a message that produces action and one that
  produces silence.
- Build templates for your most common scenarios and refine them based on response rates.
